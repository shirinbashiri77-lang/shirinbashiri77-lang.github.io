Project 01 · Prepared Oct 2026

Student lifestyle, mental health, and stress

A statistical study of how sleep, study time, social-media use, physical activity, and gender relate to student stress across 100,000 records.

Dataset100,000 student records
MethodsDescriptives, t-test, Pearson r, regression
OutcomeStress level
FormatPublication-ready report

Research story

What most clearly tracks with student stress?

The analysis moved from baseline distributions to group comparison, correlation, and adjusted prediction. With a very large sample, the interpretation deliberately prioritizes effect magnitude over p-values alone.

  • 01Activity was the strongest correlate.Physical activity had the largest bivariate association with stress (r = −.297).
  • 02Sleep remained important after adjustment.Its standardized regression effect was β = −.280.
  • 03Gender was not a useful separator.Mean stress was 4.13 for both groups; estimated d ≈ −.002.
  • 04The model informs, but does not diagnose.R² = .167, leaving most individual variation unexplained.

Project 02 · Prepared Oct 2026

AI training, maturity, and productivity

An econometric analysis of 150,000 observations nested within 10,000 companies, testing how training and investment relate to AI capability and operational productivity.

Dataset150,000 panel observations
Structure10,000 companies · 2023–2026
MethodsClustered OLS, Welch ANOVA, VIF
OutcomesAI maturity + productivity

Research story

Does AI training become operational value?

Because companies appear repeatedly in the data, the analysis used company-clustered standard errors. Nested models then separated training’s direct association from the capability and implementation pathways around it.

  • 01Training strongly tracked maturity.The bivariate relationship was r = .822; the adjusted extended effect remained β = .385.
  • 02Maturity was the productivity bridge.It was the dominant extended-model predictor at β = .519.
  • 03Execution quality mattered.Task automation was positive (β = .215), while failure rate was negative (β = −.133).
  • 04Context did not determine destiny.Industry explained 4.3% of maturity variation—far less than investment and capability factors.

Project 03 · Prepared Oct 2026

Education, gender, and income in Toronto

A census-data study of how education and gender relate to median employment income in Toronto — and how the pattern changed between 2019 and 2020.

SourceStatCan 2021 Census, Table 98-10-0410-01
Sample147,840 aggregate groups · Toronto CMA, age 25–64
MethodsGroup medians, percentage gaps, change rates
OutcomeMedian employment income ($)

Research story

Does the education premium hold for everyone?

The data is aggregated: each row is a demographic group, not one person — so the analysis compares group medians with percentage differences instead of individual-level tests. Work activity is classified in 2020 for both income years, following Statistics Canada's definition.

  • 01The bachelor’s premium widened in 2020. Bachelor’s holders earned 41.1% more than high-school graduates in 2019, and 46.7% more in 2020.
  • 02The gender gap narrowed in all 11 groups — but stayed uneven. It was widest among apprenticeship certificate holders (42.7% in 2019, 40.1% in 2020) and smallest for doctorate holders in 2020 (9.6%). More education did not consistently mean a smaller gap.
  • 03Growth was uneven across the ladder. Median incomes rose at every level in 2020, from +1.7% (no certificate) to +8.7% (bachelor’s).
  • 04The ladder isn’t strictly linear. In 2019, apprenticeship certificates ($73,000) out-earned college diplomas ($60,800).
